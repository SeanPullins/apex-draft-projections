Watchlist — college players who haven’t reached the NFL

This is not a draft class, and these are not APEX scores. These are players with a 2025 college season who have not signed with an NFL team. The 2026 season has not been played, nobody declares until January 2027, and no one has tested at a combine. The projection here answers a deliberately narrow question — how often players who produced like this have reached an NFL roster at all — and says nothing about whether a player becomes any good once he gets there. That second question needs athletic testing and draft position, which these players do not yet have, and it is the question the rest of this site is about.

What the tiers mean

Tiers are cut on how often players who produced like this have gone on to reach an NFL roster. That is a different and much more answerable question than the one the rest of this site asks. It is trained on every college player rather than only the drafted ones — nearly 35,000 of them since 2014, about one in five of whom made it — which is the population this page actually contains.

Because the tiers are probabilities rather than percentiles, they mean the same thing across positions, and a thin position is allowed to have few players in the top band instead of being forced to fill a quota.

    How this list is built

    Take everyone who played enough of a 2025 college season, adjust each player’s grade for how much he actually played, and rank him against his own position. That ranking is description, not prediction: it is what happened on the field last year, nothing more.

    The two probability columns are a separate thing and they are a forecast. A model trained on college players from 2014 onward turns the adjusted grade, workload, career shape and level of competition into a probability of reaching an NFL roster and of being drafted, and the tiers are cut on the first of those. Section 4 below describes it and section 5 scores it, including where its numbers are wrong.

    1. Who is in the pool

    Every player with a 2025 college season and at least games, who has not signed with an NFL team. Defenders need defensive snaps and offensive linemen offensive snaps, since grades on a handful of snaps are not measuring anything. That leaves players.

    Position groups come from where a player lined up in college, not from where an NFL team might eventually play him. Pool sizes vary a lot — there are several times as many defensive backs as quarterbacks — which is exactly why nothing is ranked across positions.

    2. What each position is ranked on

    One number per player, chosen to be the thing that position is paid to do:

    GroupRanked onPlaying time measured as Players

    Receivers and tight ends are ranked per route run rather than per target, because targets measure what the quarterback decided to do. Linemen have their pass-block and run-block grades averaged: a tackle who cannot do one of them is not a prospect, and splitting the two would imply a precision the percentile does not have. The underlying grades are licensed and are not published — only the percentiles derived from them.

    3. Adjusting for how much he played

    A grade earned on sixty snaps is a claim about sixty snaps. Ranking on the raw number puts part-timers with one good afternoon at the top of every position — the unadjusted list had a cornerback who ranked in the 100th percentile on grade and the 1st on volume.

    So each grade is pulled toward its position’s average by how much the player actually played:

    adjusted = w × grade + (1 − w) × position average where w = n / (n + k)

    n is the player’s playing time and k is the position’s own 25th-percentile volume. A full-season starter keeps nearly all of his grade; a fringe player is pulled most of the way back to average. Nobody is deleted for playing little — the claim is just held to the evidence behind it. This is what makes the order here differ from raw grade: it moved players by more than ten percentile points, the largest by .

    Players below the th percentile for playing time, or under games, are tagged thin sample and can be hidden with the checkbox. They are tagged rather than dropped because a good player on a bad team also plays few snaps.

    4. From production to a projection

    The adjusted grade becomes a percentile within the position group, and that percentile — along with playing time, career shape, how a player’s grade moved year over year, and whether he played in a power conference — feeds a model trained on what happened next to every college player since 2014, not just the drafted ones. Roughly one in five reaches an NFL roster, so this is an ordinary prediction problem rather than a needle in a haystack.

      The percentile alone could not order the list. A percentile has a hundred buckets and a position has hundreds of players, so the entire top of every position rounds to p100 and the order inside tier 1 would be arbitrary. With All positions selected the table sorts on that rank, which interleaves fairly — every position’s best, then every position’s second-best — rather than pretending the best cornerback and the best quarterback can be separated.

      5. Does 70% mean 70%?

      The tiers are printed as probabilities, so the honest test is not whether the model orders players well but whether its numbers are true. Those are different claims and only the second one licenses reading a percentage literally. AUC answers the first and says nothing at all about the second — a model can rank perfectly while every number it prints is wrong.

      6. Three ways of marking the same homework

      One protocol cannot rule out the two things a sceptic should suspect first about a model trained on repeated observations of the same athlete. So here are three, hardest last.

      ProtocolWhat it forbidsAUC 95% CICalibration error

      Player is the check on whether the same player appeared on both sides of a fold, carrying his eventual outcome with him. It comes out identical to season because the folds already forbade it: training is restricted to players whose college careers were over before the scored season, which excludes every row of anyone being scored. Zero overlap in all six folds.

      Mature is the one that found something. A training row's inputs were always knowable at the time, but its label was not: a player whose last college season was 2019 and who first turned up on a roster in 2023 was being counted as a success in the 2020 fold, using three years of future knowledge. Forbidding that — a row may only train once its outcome had actually resolved — costs about two points of AUC. That is the honest size of the effect, and it is why the number is quoted with the protocol attached.

      Every interval resamples players, not rows. Roughly 52,000 player-seasons come from about 23,000 people, and treating them as 52,000 independent observations would make these intervals too narrow.

      9. The mistake this model made, and what fixing it cost

      Until recently the model was trained on one row per player — his last college season, with career shape measured across the whole career. That is a sound description of a player who is finished. It is the wrong description of most of this list: roughly of college player-seasons belong to someone who will play again.

      The two are not the same row even when they carry identical numbers. “One season played” meant never played again in the training data, which is mostly a washout, and means this was his first year on the watchlist, which says nothing. Scored on players in the middle of a career, the old model lost a fifth of its discrimination and its probabilities bent both ways at once: it said 1.6% where the truth was 15.3%, and 96% where the truth was 76%.

      It is now trained on every player-season, each described only by what was knowable at the time. That halved the calibration error. It also cut the headline accuracy figure from 0.929 to , and that is not a regression — the old number was measured on finished careers, which are much easier to sort and are not what this page is full of. The lower number is the same model measured on the players it is actually asked about.

      What this page still will not tell you

      It projects whether a player arrives, not whether he is any good. Those are different questions and only the first one is answerable from here.

      Asking the second was tried and measured rather than assumed. A model given production and PFF grades but no athletic testing and no draft slot, tested on classes it never saw, reaches 0.640 AUC at predicting who becomes good; ranking on the grades alone gets 0.628. The whole model is worth about one AUC point over a list this page already publishes — while a model that knows nothing but where a player was drafted scores 0.799. There is no honest way to dress that up as a projection of NFL quality, so it is not published as one. It is worth revisiting after the February 2027 combine, when these players finally have the input that carries the model.

      A projected draft position was tried too, and dropped for the same reason. Scored walk-forward it lands about 60 picks from the truth on average and inside the right round only half the time — close to two rounds of error. “Drafted” as a probability answers the same question at 0.931, so that is what the column shows.

      One caveat on the headline numbers: looks far better than anything else on this site, and part of that is simply an easier question. The pool includes thousands of players who were never NFL prospects at all, and separating them from the ones who were is not hard. The figure worth reading is the gap over ranking on the grade percentile — about eleven AUC points — because that is what the model adds to what the page could already have told you.

      How APEX works

      APEX answers two questions from what was knowable on draft night: how likely is this player to succeed, and how likely is he to never play at all? They are not mirror images, and the second one is where the model has the clearer advantage over the draft order. It is trained on every pick from 2000–2021 and validated the only honest way — each class is scored by a model that never saw it.

      Four definitions of outcome

      • Hit — career weighted Approximate Value in the top 25% of the player’s own draft class at his position group. Position- and era-relative by construction, so a 2004 guard and a 2021 quarterback are judged fairly.
      • Starter — three or more seasons as a primary starter.
      • Pro Bowler — at least one Pro Bowl selection.
      • Bust — bottom-quartile career value among the players taken in the same draft-capital band. Judged against his own neighbourhood of the draft, not the class as a whole, so the question stays meaningful at pick 5 and at pick 205 alike.

      The headline APEX score blends the three success probabilities (50% hit, 30% starter, 20% Pro Bowl) onto a 0–100 scale. Bust risk is kept out of it, on purpose. It is a different question rather than a penalty, and folding it in would bury the one number this model is clearly better at than the draft order.

      Those three weights are a value judgement, not a finding. Nothing in the data says a top-quartile career is worth five times a Pro Bowl; that ratio encodes a preference for durable starters over rare stars, and a team building differently would be right to choose differently. The weights were never tuned against outcomes — doing so would have made the composite look better while telling you less. The three probabilities underneath are published separately on every player card precisely so you can apply your own weighting, and the model would not need retraining if you did.

      Where the model actually beats the draft order

      Draft position is consensus upside — it is the league agreeing about who is exciting, and it is very hard to improve on. Held out, APEX gains about one AUC point over the draft-slot prior on predicting who becomes good, and on classes it has never seen that edge shrinks to almost nothing.

      Predicting who disappoints for his draft slot is a different question, and the market prices it far worse — draft position barely predicts it at all, by construction. On held-out classes the model gains +6.0 AUC points there against +1.1 on finding hits, the largest edge measured anywhere in this project.

      With the same caveat as everything else here: that edge is a backtest. On the 2022–2023 classes, scored before those players had played, the model is behind the draft-slot prior on bust by 2.4 AUC points. There are only 80 busts across those two classes — nowhere near enough to settle it either way — but the backtest advantage has not appeared out of sample, exactly as it has not for the hit label. Both numbers are on the Insights tab.

      With one blunt exception: the model cannot tell you which first-rounders bust. On picks 1–32 its edge is +0.5 AUC points with a confidence interval straddling zero — a coin flip. About a quarter of first-round picks return less than the players taken around them, and this model has no idea which quarter. Its skill on that question starts at pick 33 and grows from there (+7.9 points for picks 33–105, +8.4 beyond 105).

      So: use the APEX score to see who the model likes, and the bust-risk column to see who it is worried about — but treat bust risk in round one as a base rate, not a read.

      The model, in one paragraph

      A smooth spline-logistic curve fit to draft position and position group forms the market prior — the baseline any model must beat, because draft slot is the strongest single predictor of NFL outcomes. On top of it, a bagged LightGBM ensemble adds four evidence blocks: athleticism (Relative Athletic Score, combine tests, size-for-position, age), college production (final-season and career stats, 2014+), PFF college grades (position-matched grades, 2014+), and market structure (pick value curves, positional draft depth). The ensemble is shrunk halfway back toward the prior in log-odds space — the model only moves off the market consensus when the evidence is strong — and every probability is isotonic-calibrated on held-out predictions.

      Ablating those blocks is unflattering and worth publishing anyway — but it has to be done in the right era. PFF college grades exist only from 2015, so an ablation pooled across 2003–2021 averages a live modern signal against a decade of rows where the column is simply blank. Across the whole window PFF is present for 29% of training rows; from 2015 it is present for 90%.

      Measured on the 2018–2021 folds, where the coverage is real, each block added to the draft-slot prior is worth:

      Block addedHit AUCGainBust AUCGain

      The grades are the largest single contributor to finding hits — more than twice what athleticism and production add together — which is the opposite of what the pooled number suggested and much closer to what anyone who watches football would expect. An earlier version of this page reported PFF at +0.1 AUC points, and that figure was an artefact of measuring a feature over years in which it did not exist.

      Training only on the 2015–2021 classes, where PFF is nearly complete, was tried and is worse: 0.8120 on hit against 0.8168, and 0.6231 on bust against 0.6272. Dropping three quarters of the rows costs more than the dilution does, and the gradient booster handles the missing years without help. The fix was to measure the ablation honestly, not to shrink the training window.

      One wrinkle worth naming: plain isotonic calibration is a step function, and it flattens a 250-player class onto about 25 plateaus — which would put four players in a dead heat at the top of every board. APEX keeps the isotonic fit but interpolates between the plateau centres, so the curve stays monotone and honest while every player the model actually separated keeps a distinct number. Checked leave-one-year-out, the smoothed version calibrates as well as the step version and ranks strictly better.

      How much of this is just the draft?

      Draft position has always been two things here at once: the baseline APEX is judged against, and one of the features APEX is built from. So it is fair to ask whether the model knows anything at all, or is simply re-ranking the league’s own order. To find out, a third model is trained on only the player — athletic testing, college production, PFF grades, age, size — and told nothing about where he was picked.

      The split is the honest summary of this project. On who becomes good, the draft wins and it is not close: strip the pick number out and the model falls apart, while adding it back is worth several times more than every scouting input combined. Whatever NFL teams know by draft night — medicals, interviews, film, scheme fit — most of it never reaches a spreadsheet.

      On who busts relative to his slot, it flips: the player-only model beats draft position outright, and knowing the pick adds almost nothing on top. That result comes with a caveat worth stating plainly — bust is defined within a draft-capital band, so the label deliberately removes most of the draft-slot signal and the comparison is not a fair fight. But the information is real: testing and production do say something about who fails to justify his selection.

      One more number puts it in perspective. The rank correlation between the player-only score and actual draft position is about −0.50, meaning that from the same raw material teams use, this model reconstructs roughly a quarter of the variance in draft order. The other half of what decides where a player goes is invisible from here.

      Tiers, and how much the score is worth

      Two players a tenth of a point apart are not meaningfully separated, but a ranked list implies they are. So each APEX score carries a standard error, measured by refitting the model two hundred times on resampled draft history — the spread of scores it would have produced had it seen a slightly different sample. The resampling draws whole draft classes rather than individual players: men in the same class share an era, a rule set, a testing regime and one labelling pass, so treating five thousand players as five thousand independent observations would understate how much the score could have moved.

      Tiers follow from that. Reading down the board, a player joins the current tier while his score sits within one standard error of the tier leader’s, and opens a new tier once he is clearly below. The comparison is always against the tier leader rather than the previous player: chaining neighbour to neighbour would swallow the entire board into a single tier, because every player is close to the one below him.

      Read tiers as the real resolution of the board. Order within a tier is noise; order between tiers is the model actually saying something.

      Would using it have produced better players?

      Every other number here is a classification metric. AUC says the model orders players better than the draft does; it does not say a team using it would have ended up with more football. So: at each historical pick, compare the player the team actually took against the best APEX player among those who demonstrably went within the next twenty selections — men who were provably available at that moment, using only the score a model that never saw that class would have produced.

      In the 2003–2021 backtest it works. A team drafting for a specific position gains +1.0 career AV per pick (CI +0.4 to +1.6), about a 5% improvement. Ignoring position entirely gains +4.0, but no team drafts that way, so the smaller number is the honest one.

      It does not survive out of sample. On the 2022–2023 classes — the ones the model never saw — the same simulation returns −0.01 AV, confidence interval −0.34 to +0.30. Careers are shorter there, so a real effect would show up smaller: scaling the backtest gain by mean career length predicts about +0.5 AV. The observed interval excludes that. This is not merely a small sample failing to reach significance — the forward data actively contradicts the backtest. On the evidence available, there is no demonstrated decision value.

      The swaps it would have made are worth seeing, because they explain the pattern. Its biggest wins are all avoided busts near the top of round one — Terrell Suggs over Johnathan Sullivan, J.J. Watt over Blaine Gabbert, Luke Kuechly over Justin Blackmon. Its worst mistakes are passing on late superstars — Aaron Rodgers, Bobby Wagner, Matthew Stafford. That asymmetry is the same one the AUC shows: this model knows more about who fails than about who becomes great.

      Honest limitations

      • Draft capital dominates. The model’s edge over the market prior is real but measured in single AUC points — anyone claiming huge edges over draft position is fooling you or themselves.
      • College production and PFF grades only exist from 2014, and PFF coverage is offense-heavy. Older classes and defenders lean more on the market + athleticism blocks.
      • The 2022–2025 boards are true out-of-training projections, and their listed outcomes are careers in progress, not final grades. On that forward test APEX and the draft order are running level on finding hits, and APEX is slightly behind on bust. Neither backtest edge has shown up out of sample yet. The samples are small — 1,016 players for hits, 80 busts — but nothing here should be read as validated by the forward test.
      • Bust risk is judged on players whose careers have had time to fail. A rookie who has not started yet is not a bust, so the forward-test bust figures use the 2022–2023 classes only.
      • Probabilities are calibrated group-wise, not prophecy. A 70% hit probability misses three times in ten — that is the job description.
