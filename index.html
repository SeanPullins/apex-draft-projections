How APEX works

APEX answers two questions from what was knowable on draft night: how likely is this player to succeed, and how likely is he to never play at all? They are not mirror images, and the second one is where the model has the clearer advantage over the draft order. It is trained on every pick from 2000–2021 and validated the only honest way — each class is scored by a model that never saw it.

Four definitions of outcome

  • Hit — career weighted Approximate Value in the top 25% of the player’s own draft class at his position group. Position- and era-relative by construction, so a 2004 guard and a 2021 quarterback are judged fairly.
  • Starter — three or more seasons as a primary starter.
  • Pro Bowler — at least one Pro Bowl selection.
  • Bust — bottom-quartile career value among the players taken in the same draft-capital band. Judged against his own neighbourhood of the draft, not the class as a whole, so the question stays meaningful at pick 5 and at pick 205 alike.

The headline APEX score blends the three success probabilities (50% hit, 30% starter, 20% Pro Bowl) onto a 0–100 scale. Bust risk is kept out of it, on purpose. It is a different question rather than a penalty, and folding it in would bury the one number this model is clearly better at than the draft order.

Where the model actually beats the draft order

Draft position is consensus upside — it is the league agreeing about who is exciting, and it is very hard to improve on. Held out, APEX gains about one AUC point over the draft-slot prior on predicting who becomes good, and on classes it has never seen that edge shrinks to almost nothing.

Predicting who disappoints for his draft slot is a different question, and the market prices it far worse — draft position barely predicts it at all, by construction. On held-out classes the model gains +6.0 AUC points there against +1.1 on finding hits, the largest edge measured anywhere in this project.

With the same caveat as everything else here: that edge is a backtest. On the 2022–2023 classes, scored before those players had played, the model is behind the draft-slot prior on bust by 2.4 AUC points. There are only 80 busts across those two classes — nowhere near enough to settle it either way — but the backtest advantage has not appeared out of sample, exactly as it has not for the hit label. Both numbers are on the Insights tab.

With one blunt exception: the model cannot tell you which first-rounders bust. On picks 1–32 its edge is +0.5 AUC points with a confidence interval straddling zero — a coin flip. About a quarter of first-round picks return less than the players taken around them, and this model has no idea which quarter. Its skill on that question starts at pick 33 and grows from there (+7.9 points for picks 33–105, +8.4 beyond 105).

So: use the APEX score to see who the model likes, and the bust-risk column to see who it is worried about — but treat bust risk in round one as a base rate, not a read.

The model, in one paragraph

A smooth spline-logistic curve fit to draft position and position group forms the market prior — the baseline any model must beat, because draft slot is the strongest single predictor of NFL outcomes. On top of it, a bagged LightGBM ensemble adds four evidence blocks: athleticism (Relative Athletic Score, combine tests, size-for-position, age), college production (final-season and career stats, 2014+), PFF college grades (position-matched grades, 2014+), and market structure (pick value curves, positional draft depth). The ensemble is shrunk halfway back toward the prior in log-odds space — the model only moves off the market consensus when the evidence is strong — and every probability is isotonic-calibrated on held-out predictions.

Ablating those blocks is unflattering and worth publishing anyway. Added to the draft-slot prior on its own, athleticism is worth about +1.0 AUC points; college production is worth +0.1, PFF grades +0.1, and adding third-party scouting consensus makes the model slightly worse. Removing athleticism costs more than ten times what removing college production costs. Whatever this model knows that the draft order does not, it learned almost entirely from workout and size data — not from tape.

One wrinkle worth naming: plain isotonic calibration is a step function, and it flattens a 250-player class onto about 25 plateaus — which would put four players in a dead heat at the top of every board. APEX keeps the isotonic fit but interpolates between the plateau centres, so the curve stays monotone and honest while every player the model actually separated keeps a distinct number. Checked leave-one-year-out, the smoothed version calibrates as well as the step version and ranks strictly better.

Tiers, and how much the score is worth

Two players a tenth of a point apart are not meaningfully separated, but a ranked list implies they are. So each APEX score carries a standard error, measured by resampling the training set forty times with replacement and refitting — the spread of scores the same model would have produced had it seen a slightly different sample of draft history.

Tiers follow from that. Reading down the board, a player joins the current tier while his score sits within one standard error of the tier leader’s, and opens a new tier once he is clearly below. The comparison is always against the tier leader rather than the previous player: chaining neighbour to neighbour would swallow the entire board into a single tier, because every player is close to the one below him.

Read tiers as the real resolution of the board. Order within a tier is noise; order between tiers is the model actually saying something.

Honest limitations

  • Draft capital dominates. The model’s edge over the market prior is real but measured in single AUC points — anyone claiming huge edges over draft position is fooling you or themselves.
  • College production and PFF grades only exist from 2014, and PFF coverage is offense-heavy. Older classes and defenders lean more on the market + athleticism blocks.
  • The 2022–2025 boards are true out-of-training projections, and their listed outcomes are careers in progress, not final grades. On that forward test APEX and the draft order are running level on finding hits, and APEX is slightly behind on bust. Neither backtest edge has shown up out of sample yet. The samples are small — 1,016 players for hits, 80 busts — but nothing here should be read as validated by the forward test.
  • Bust risk is judged on players whose careers have had time to fail. A rookie who has not started yet is not a bust, so the forward-test bust figures use the 2022–2023 classes only.
  • Probabilities are calibrated group-wise, not prophecy. A 70% hit probability misses three times in ten — that is the job description.
